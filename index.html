The Calculus of Justice

1. The Mathematics of Inequality

How do we measure if a society is fair? Economists use a tool called the Lorenz Curve to graph the distribution of wealth. The x-axis represents the percentage of the population (from poorest to richest), and the y-axis represents the percentage of total wealth they hold.

If wealth were distributed perfectly equally, the bottom 20% of people would hold exactly 20% of the wealth, forming a perfectly straight line: y = x.

In reality, the curve sags downward. The area between the line of perfect equality and the actual Lorenz curve represents total inequality. We calculate this area using definite integrals to find the Gini Coefficient.

The Gini Formula

The Gini coefficient is exactly twice the area between the line of equality y = x and the Lorenz curve L(x), evaluated from 0 to 1:

G = 2 ∫01 [x - L(x)] dx

3. The Veil of Ignorance

Put away the calculator and put on the hat of a philosopher. In 1971, John Rawls introduced a thought experiment called the "Veil of Ignorance."

Imagine you are waiting to be born into either Norway or South Africa, but you are behind a "veil"—you have absolutely no idea if you will be born rich, poor, healthy, sick, or into a minority group. Rawls argued that from this blind position, people would naturally design a society that protects the least advantaged, just in case they end up at the bottom.

4. Defining Justice: The Missing Variables

We used the Gini coefficient as a mathematical proxy for "justice." But mathematics has blind spots when it comes to the human experience.

The Freedom Dilemma

Imagine two newly formed societies:

  • Society X achieves a near-perfect Gini of 0.20, but the government achieves this by strictly assigning jobs and capping all salaries.
  • Society Y has a highly unequal Gini of 0.45, but guarantees absolute freedom to choose your career, innovate, and keep your earnings.

Which society is more "just"?
